Reading 1

What happened when Irish taxpayers paid artists £280 a week for a year

The last few years haven’t been easy for Conor Matthews. As a writer and creative struggling to meet his basic needs, he found himself on benefits. He couldn’t find any job in his profession, so he started working as a night porter at a hotel. The job was so tiring that he had little energy left to follow his passions. “I was feeling extremely stressed,” he says. But he got a second chance. The Irish government chose him to take part in a basic income pilot programme for artists.

More than 9,000 people applied and 2,000 were randomly chosen to receive £280 (€325/$375) a week for three years, from August 2022 to 2025. There were no rules about how the money should be spent.

When Matthews started getting the money, he quit his job as a porter and was able to spend more time on his projects. He says the past year has been the “most productive, educational and hopeful” of his life.

Reports published by the Irish government showed that people who received the payments felt less stress and depression. They worked fewer hours in other jobs and spent more time and money on their art.

More ideas about UBI

Reading 2

Why might UBI become more relevant?

Cleo Goodman, a basic income expert at the Autonomy think tank, says that the growth of AI has created worries that robots could replace many jobs. According to Goodman, if people received a basic income, they would have more freedom to choose work that matters and have more flexibility in their lives.

Guy Standing, an economist and co-founder of the Basic Income Earth Network, says the world of work has changed in the last several decades. Many people have temporary work contracts and don’t have as much security as workers in the past.

He says the biggest and most common result of UBI programmes around the world is that people feel less stressed and less depressed. Another finding is that people who take part in the programme work more, not less. In Ireland, people did more of the work they were trained and qualified to do and fewer things that they did “just to survive”.

Most people who support the programmes hope they will lead to a universal basic income, where every adult receives a regular monthly payment which they can spend however they like. Critics worry that giving all members of society free money could make them buy more products and services, causing prices to rise. However, Standing says that if businesses are able to produce enough goods, prices might not go up.

Final speaking task

Choose one option, then explain your answer using the grammar from both readings.

Try to use: few, fewer, little, less, any, no, much, many, most, several, another, enough, every, all.

1. Who should receive UBI?

Use: every / few / all / most
Explain who should be included and who should not.

2. What would happen to working hours?

Use: fewer / less / any
Would that be a positive change?

3. How much money should UBI provide?

Use: much / enough / little / all
Name the costs that should be covered.

4. Which result is more likely?

Use: less / more / much / many
Use ideas from the readings.

5. If UBI were tested on one group first, who should it be?

Use: another / several / most / many
Explain why that group should be prioritised.

6. Should there be rules about how UBI is spent?

Use: no / few / any
What rules would be acceptable?

7. Which statement is closer to your opinion?

Use: most / many / few / no
Why do you think so?

8. If a UBI programme caused problems, what should happen?

Use: several / all / another / no
What would you change first?

9. Which problem is more serious today?

Use: few / little / fewer / less
Give an example from real life.

10. Would UBI work in your country?

Use: few / many / enough / most / all
Give at least two concrete reasons.
